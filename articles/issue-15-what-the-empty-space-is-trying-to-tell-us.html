What the Empty Space Is Trying to Tell Us

Published August 14, 2026 · Brandon Labonte, SHRM-CP

Sometimes the most responsible thing a recruiter can send a hiring manager is nothing.

That is a difficult sentence to write when you lead recruiting.

We have open requisitions. Hiring managers need people. The business needs people. My team wants to deliver.

And sometimes, after doing the work, asking the deeper questions, and looking honestly at the pipeline, the answer is still this.

We don’t have the right person yet.

That can be uncomfortable. Especially when the requisitions start piling up.

There is pressure to produce something. Send another candidate. Schedule another interview. Give the hiring manager another option. Maybe they will see something we missed. Maybe the candidate will surprise us. Sometimes that happens. But this week, we pulled our reports and the data told us something we probably already knew.

We had activity. What we did not have enough of was qualified activity. Candidates were entering the pipeline, but the deeper we went into conversations, the more we realized some of them simply were not meeting the standard of the requisition.

And that is where things got interesting for me. Because the deeper questions were working. They were doing exactly what I believe better recruiting conversations should do. They were helping us understand people before we moved them farther into the process.

Why are you considering a change? What are you really looking for? What would have to be different this time? What does success actually look like to you?

Those questions do not always produce another interview. Sometimes they produce clarity. Sometimes a candidate realizes the opportunity is not what they actually want. Sometimes we realize the experience is not there. Sometimes what looked like alignment on a résumé starts looking very different after a real conversation. And sometimes the result of a good recruiting conversation is that someone leaves the pipeline.

That might not look like progress on a report. But maybe it is.

I have been reading James Clear’s #AtomicHabits, and one line has been following me around lately.

“Goals are good for setting a direction, but systems are best for making progress.”

That hit differently this week. Recruiting certainly has goals. Fill the position. Find the right person. Make the hire. Support the business. Improve retention. But our systems determine what we are willing to do when reaching those goals becomes difficult.

It is easy to protect a hiring standard when your pipeline is full of strong candidates.

It gets much harder when the requisitions are growing and the people you want simply are not there yet. That is when the temptation to confuse activity with progress becomes real.

Send the C candidate. Send the low B candidate.

At least we are giving the hiring manager someone. We did some of that this week. And the feedback came back pretty much the way we expected.

Not enough experience. Not the right fit. Not strong enough for the role.

That made me turn the questions back toward myself. Maybe an empty pipeline is not always a sourcing problem.

Maybe it is feedback.

Is the expectation realistic? Are we searching in the right places? Does the compensation match the talent we are trying to attract? Are we describing the opportunity accurately? Are we asking for something the market simply is not giving us right now? Do we need to change something? Or do we simply need enough discipline to keep looking without lowering the standard?

Those are harder questions than asking how many candidates we submitted this week. They are also probably more useful. And strangely enough, this entire thought started somewhere completely different. Monday afternoon arrived and I still had not posted anything on LinkedIn.

Apparently, LinkedIn survived.

I had ideas. I could have written something. But everything I came up with felt like I was filling an empty space simply because the calendar said something was supposed to be there. So I left it empty for a while. That made me think about how uncomfortable we become with empty spaces. An empty spot on a content calendar. An open position on a recruiting report. A quiet moment after asking someone a difficult question. A candidate pipeline without enough qualified people in it.

Our instinct is often to fill the space. But maybe we should not always be so quick. Silence after a difficult question might mean someone is actually thinking. An empty pipeline might mean our screening process is protecting the standard.

An open requisition might be telling us something about the market, our expectations, our compensation, or the opportunity itself.

The empty space might contain information.

Leading recruiters is making me think about this differently too. My responsibility cannot only be creating urgency.

I want my team to move. I want them to source. I want them to follow up. I want them to ask better questions. I want them to understand the needs of our hiring managers and the needs of the business.

But I also want them to develop the confidence to say something that can be much harder. We don’t have the right person yet. Then we figure out why. Maybe that is what a good recruiting system should help us do. Not simply create more activity. Create better information. ->Better conversations. ->Better decisions. And occasionally, the confidence to leave a space empty until we understand what actually belongs there.

An empty space is not always something waiting to be filled. Sometimes it is information. The question is whether we are willing to listen before we rush to make it disappear.

Better conversations. Better hiring outcomes.
