Even a Thoroughbred Needs a Harness

Published July 10, 2026 · Brandon Labonte, SHRM-CP

I have been overextended this week.

Not in a dramatic way. Nothing is falling apart. There have simply been more responsibilities, conversations, ideas, and problems competing for my attention than I have had the capacity to handle well.

I do not always like admitting that.

I am driven. I like solving problems. I like being dependable. When I see something that needs to be improved, my instinct is usually to start working on it. But capacity is real.

Even a thoroughbred needs a harness. (I'm a thoroughbred)

Not because it lacks strength. Not because it has lost its drive. The harness helps direct the power so the horse does not exhaust itself running in every direction.

That has been one of my lessons this week.

Drive is a gift, but drive without reflection can become hurry. Ambition without discipline can become scattered energy. A full schedule can create the appearance of progress while some of the most important work remains unfinished. This shows up in recruiting all the time.

A hiring manager feels urgency, so a position gets opened before the team has clearly defined what success looks like. A recruiter feels pressure, so conversations become shorter, questions become more transactional, and activity begins replacing understanding. A candidate feels dissatisfied, so they start applying everywhere before slowing down long enough to understand what they are actually trying to change.

Everyone is moving, but movement is not always progress.

That is where process matters. Process is sometimes treated like unnecessary administration. Another form. Another meeting. Another step standing between us and the work. Poor process can certainly become that.

But a good process protects the thinking that pressure tends to remove.

It reminds the hiring manager to define the problem before requesting candidates. It reminds the recruiter to understand motivation before presenting an opportunity. It creates a place to record risks, concerns, expectations, and unanswered questions before they become problems later. It helps us slow down enough to notice what the conversation is actually teaching us.

Leadership follows process to create habit.

That does not mean leaders blindly follow a checklist. It means they build enough structure around important work that doing it well does not depend entirely on memory, energy, or how busy the week becomes. The process creates repetition. Repetition creates habit. Habit creates consistency.

And consistency makes better conversations more likely, even when capacity is stretched.

I have been documenting more of my day-to-day conversations lately. Candidate conversations. Hiring manager conversations. Leadership conversations. Sometimes even quick exchanges that seemed small until I thought about them later.

I am doing that because growth can disappear when we do not capture it.

We have the conversation. We feel the lesson. We tell ourselves we will remember it. Then the week gets busy, another problem takes its place, and the same lesson returns two weeks later wearing different clothes.

Systems preserve the thinking.

They do not replace judgment, listening, awareness, or leadership. They make it easier to use those things consistently.

I am still learning how to manage my own capacity. I am still learning that saying yes to every worthwhile idea does not mean I am leading well. Sometimes leadership means slowing down, choosing the next right thing, and trusting the process enough to keep moving one step at a time.

One conversation. One lesson. One pattern. One brick at a time.

Better conversations do not happen because we are naturally gifted communicators or because we care more than everyone else. They are built through attention, humility, repetition, and the discipline to keep learning from the people in front of us.

Even a thoroughbred needs a harness.

Sometimes the structure is not holding us back. It is helping our strength carry us somewhere that matters.
